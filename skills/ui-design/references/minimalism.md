# Flat Design & Minimalism

Ornament removed. No bevels, no textures, no simulated depth. Hierarchy comes from space, scale, weight, and a small palette, so the content is the only thing decorated.

**Ask first (`AskUserQuestion`)**: whether the content is strong enough to carry the
page alone (minimalism has nowhere to hide weak content); whether the product needs to
look distinctive or trustworthy first; and whether the team can hold the discipline,
because this style degrades faster than any other when features accrete.

## Flat and minimal are not the same thing

- **Flat** is a rendering decision: no simulated depth. A flat interface can still be dense, loud, and colourful.
- **Minimal** is an editorial decision: fewer elements. A minimal interface can still use shadows and texture.

Most products described as minimalist are flat plus restraint. Keeping the two apart matters, because "make it minimal" usually means "remove elements", and that is a content decision the designer cannot make alone.

## What carries the hierarchy

With ornament gone, four tools do all the work, and each has to be used deliberately:

- **Space.** The primary tool. Proximity groups things; distance separates them. A consistent spacing scale (4 or 8px base, then 8/12/16/24/32/48/64) is not a nicety here, it is the structure.
- **Type scale and weight.** One family is usually enough. Two or three sizes and two weights outrank five sizes at one weight, because the jumps have to be obvious to read as hierarchy.
- **A small palette.** One accent that means something (interactive, or the primary action) plus a neutral ramp. An accent used decoratively stops signalling anything.
- **Alignment.** With no boxes and borders, edges are what the eye follows. A broken alignment is visible immediately, which is both the risk and the discipline.

## What it costs

**Affordance.** This is the real bill. Flat design removed the cues that said "clickable", and the fix was a decade of convention: underlined links, an accent colour reserved for actions, buttons with a fill. Break those conventions and users stop finding the controls. A flat, borderless, unfilled "ghost button" in a neutral colour is a control many people will not see.

**Nowhere to hide.** Ornament covers weak spacing, mediocre type, and unclear content. Minimalism does not. It demands better copy, better photography, and a stricter grid, so it is often the more expensive style to do well despite looking like less work.

**Sameness.** The default sans-serif, generous white space, one blue accent, and a centred hero is the safest and most anonymous interface available. Executed without a point of view, minimal reads as unfinished rather than confident.

**Density conflicts.** Minimalism prefers space; professional tools need information per screen. A trader, a radiologist, and an ops engineer all need density that a minimal aesthetic resists. Do not spend three screens of scrolling on what belonged in one table.

## When it fits

| Situation | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Content-led products: reading, writing, media | Strong. Get out of the content's way |
| Products with genuinely few actions | Strong |
| Design systems spanning many products | Strong. It tokenises cleanly and scales |
| A brand whose whole claim is restraint | Strong |
| Dense professional tools | Careful. Keep flat, drop the space |
| A brand that must feel distinctive fast | Weak alone. Needs one committed decision to carry it |

## Making it not anonymous

Minimalism gets its personality from **one committed choice**, not from many small ones:

1. **A typeface with a view.** The single highest-leverage decision. A distinctive grotesque, a real serif, or a mono display face changes the entire feel while nothing else moves.
2. **An unusual accent.** Move off the default blue. One confident, slightly wrong colour does more than a gradient.
3. **An asymmetric or unusually wide grid.** Off-centre reads as deliberate; centred reads as default.
4. **One deliberate detail**, repeated: a rule weight, a corner treatment, a hairline that appears everywhere.

Pick one and hold it everywhere. Four competing gestures produce noise, which is the failure mode minimalism exists to avoid.
